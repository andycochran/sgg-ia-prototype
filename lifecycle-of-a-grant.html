Lifecycle of a Grant

The grant process follows a linear lifecycle that includes creating the funding opportunity, applying, making award decisions, and successfully implementing the award. The specific actions along the lifecycle are grouped into three main phases:

  • Pre-award phase - Funding opportunities and application review
  • Award phase - Award decisions and notifications
  • Post-award phase - Implementation, reporting, and closeout

Pre-Award Phase

The pre-award phase is the first stage of the grant lifecycle. It consists of 3 steps:

  1. Agencies announce funding opportunities
  2. Applicants submit proposals
  3. Agencies review proposals

Agencies announce funding opportunities

Agencies write and publish a Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) based on related legislation and their budget. A NOFO gives you the information you need to find out if you are eligible and if the opportunity is a good fit. You can find these announcements by searching for funding opportunities.

Applicants submit proposals

Before you start filling out an application, take some time to understand the application process. Getting ready to apply involves planning. Federal funding is competitive, and good planning helps you compete.

The planning process varies by grant type. But it usually includes a few key steps: understand the process, develop your project idea, do research, write your proposal, and complete the application.

Agencies review proposals

Once the application deadline passes, the awarding agency starts reviewing submissions. The review process varies by grant type, but it usually includes these steps:

Initial screening

During the first screening, the agency checks that your application has all required parts. The required parts vary by grant, but they often include proof of eligibility, a program narrative, and a budget. If your application is missing a required part, it will likely be rejected before further review.

Programmatic review

Applications that pass this first check get a deeper review. Reviewers assess the technical and programmatic quality of each one. For many grants, independent experts conduct this review, using a scoring system set by the agency.

A common method is a peer review panel. At least three reviewers score each application on their own. Then the panel meets to discuss each application's strengths and weaknesses. Agencies follow rules to keep this process fair — these rules help prevent conflicts of interest among reviewers. Federal staff oversee and take part in this process.

Financial review

Federal agencies review each budget line item closely. They also look at your total proposed budget to help ensure it follows financial rules. The review also compares your requested amount to the total funding available for the program.

Award Phase

Once the federal agency finishes reviewing applications, the award phase begins. Only federal agency staff can make the final award decisions. These staff are trusted to manage public funds, and they have the legal authority to sign binding agreements.

They review the programmatic and financial reviews of each application. Then they make award recommendations based on those reviews. Several levels within the agency review these recommendations. This helps ensure the decisions are high-quality, fair, and unbiased.

Notice of Award

Once the agency makes its final award decisions, it sends a Notice of Award, or NOA, to each selected entity. The NOA is the official, legally binding award notice.

You accept the grant by signing the grant agreement or by drawing down funds. Once you accept, you're legally bound to meet all the terms and conditions of the grant.

As a recipient, you must also follow federal laws, rules, and policies that apply to grants. Visit Grant policies to learn more.

Post-Award Phase

The post award phase covers the grant's full award period. This phase includes carrying out the grant, reporting your progress, and completing closeout. The federal agency that awards your grant is also there to help you meet the grant's terms and conditions. Your job is to carry out the grant program faithfully and diligently.

Reporting

The agency monitors your progress and spending. It does this through programmatic and financial reports, as well as performance metrics set in your grant agreement. Most recipients carry out their grants ethically and efficiently. Still, these checks matter — they keep the process transparent and help prevent fraud and abuse.

Your awarding agency typically assigns a grants management officer and a program officer to your grant. You'll work with both throughout the life of the grant. They review your reports and may conduct site visits, so we recommend building a strong relationship with them. It's better to talk with your grant and program officers to clarify terms or expectations up front than to wait for problems to surface in a report.

Reporting requirements, schedules, and systems vary by grant. Review your grant's terms and conditions carefully for this information.

Auditing

Federal grant-making agencies and grant recipients are both audited. The Government Accountability Office, the Office of Inspector General, and other federal offices monitor and review agency policies, spending, and activities. These same offices also monitor and review how recipients perform.

Single Audits

If your organization is required to have a single audit, the federal awarding agency makes sure it happens. Your organization then submits the audit report to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse on time. The agency also follows up on audit findings. It confirms your organization takes the right corrective action. The agency has 6 months after the Clearinghouse accepts your report to issue a decision on any findings.

Does your organization need a single audit? You may need one if you spend $750,000 or more in federal awards during your fiscal year. This audit takes the place of other federal financial audits required by law. After the audit is done, you submit the report to the Clearinghouse. You have 9 months after the audit period ends to do this. You must also follow up and correct any audit findings.

Closeout

Closeout is the final step in the grant process. To complete closeout, you must submit your final financial and programmatic reports. Under OMB Uniform Grants Guidance §200.343, you have 90 days after your grant expires or ends to submit these reports. The awarding agency then reviews them. It checks that you met all grant terms and spent the funds correctly.

How do you know when a grant is officially over? The awarding agency must confirm that you've completed all required grant work and administrative tasks. Until the agency confirms this, you're still responsible for meeting all the grant's terms. Closeout can take several months if there are financial questions to resolve.

If you bought property using grant funds, closeout is also when you must handle that property exactly as the grant requires. This includes completing any required property reports. Finally, you're usually required to keep your grant records for at least 3 years from the date of your final expenditure report.
