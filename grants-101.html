Grants 101

A grant is one of the government's tools for funding projects. These projects provide public services, boost the economy, and benefit the public. Grants fund many kinds of work, such as research, recovery efforts, and infrastructure building.

Grant Programs

If you're new to grants, finding the right program can feel overwhelming. Grant programs fall into 2 types: federal and non-federal. Each type offers a range of funding sources and program types.

Federal programs

If you’re seeking a federal grant for your organization, you can use the search tool on Simpler to filter, sort, and find funding opportunities that fit your needs.

SAM.gov Assistance Listings is also a reliable source for federal program information. These programs offer grants, loans, scholarships, insurance, and other types of assistance.

Non-federal programs

Many nonprofits and for-profit businesses also offer grants or other funding. The Community Foundations Locator is a good place to start. It lists grant-makers in your area.

Programs for personal benefits

Are you looking for a grant to help with personal costs, like a small business, rent, home repairs, or medical bills? Grants.gov probably isn't the right site for you. But you can find these opportunities through other sites and programs:

USA.gov
This site lists state and federal agencies and programs. It also offers access to the benefits and services, including personal funding.

GovLoans.gov
This site is helpful if you’re looking for any type of personal or business loan.

StudentAid.gov
This is the place to look for education-related funding.

Types of Grants

Block grant

The federal government usually awards block grants to U.S. state or territory governments, but some go directly to local governments.

Block grant recipients carry out programs within broad purposes and rules set by legislation. They differ from discretionary grants because they give states more freedom in how they run the program. States can use block grant funds to start a program themselves, or they can make sub-awards to local organizations to provide services in their area. As long as recipients meet the purpose and rules set by law, they can decide how to use the funding.

Discretionary grant

With discretionary grants, a federal agency chooses a recipient based on merit and eligibility. After you submit an application, the agency reviews it through a competitive process and makes a final funding decision.

The agency reviews and scores the quality of each application. It bases its funding decision on the application’s quality and how well the project fits the program’s mission. This is a competitive process.

Mandatory grant

Mandatory grants must go to every eligible applicant, usually a government entity. Congress sets the rules for these grants in a law called the authorizing statute.

This law defines:

  • The purpose of the grant program
  • The minimum eligibility requirements for applicants
  • Often, the grant amount

The law also names a federal agency to administer, or manage, the program. This covers the grant’s full lifecycle, from posting the opportunity to awarding the grant to closing it out.

If an entity applies and meets the minimum requirements, the federal agency must award the grant. This differs from discretionary grants, which are awarded competitively.

Depending on the program, state or local governments may use mandatory grant funds to issue sub-awards. For example, a state might use the funds to build a road or support public education.

Formula Grant

A formula grant is a type of mandatory grant awarded using statistical criteria for specific kinds of work. Legislation and regulations define these criteria, as well as how much funding is distributed. The word formula refers to how the funding is calculated and distributed.

Medicaid is an example of a formula grant. By law, the federal government matches state funds for activities covered by Medicaid. States must follow the regulations and provide the required services. In return, they receive matching funds based on the formula.

Formula grants are noncompetitive. The formula is set by law, so funds must be awarded according to that formula. These grants usually go to state, local, or territory governments, not to private organizations. Individuals don’t receive formula grants directly.

Cooperative Agreement

Cooperative agreements are similar to grants, but with one key difference. Federal rules say a cooperative agreement involves “substantial involvement” between the federal agency and the recipient. This means the agency plays an active role in carrying out the project, not just a funding role.

What does substantial involvement mean in practice? It’s about how directly federal employees take part in running the program. With a grant, the federal government mostly oversees and monitors the work from a distance. With a cooperative agreement, federal employees work more closely alongside the recipient. Think of “cooperative” as working side-by-side. The exact level of involvement varies by program and agency.
